Goal

To lay out the evidence for the Deakin/Graeff hypothesis that had been accumulated over the preceding 5 years.

Summary

The Deakin/Graeff hypothesis states that the DRN acts to promote learned fear and suppress innate fear, while the MRN acts to prevent the formation of maladaptive behavior under chronic stress. Supporting this hypothesis, Graeff et al present results showing that injection of 8-OH-DPAT into the DRN impairs learned fear in mice, while injection of FG-7142 or kainic-acid into DRN, or systemic administration of D-fenfluramine enhanced it. Kainic acid additionally impaired active escape. They elaborated on this with human experiments showing that conditioned fear is increased by D-fenfluramine, ritanserin and SR-46349B, while innate fear is reduced by D-fenfluramine and ritanserin.

To support the role of the MRN they presented studies indicating increased ulcer formation in MRN-lesioned rats undergoing chronic stress. Additionally highly resilient rats had stronger responses to systemic 5-MeO-DMT, and 8-OH-DPAT improved resilience to chronic stress in rats (they hypothesized that the resilience promoting effects of the MRN come from 5HT1A receptors). (This could also be explained by silencing of DRN though, according to learned-helplessness-insights-from-neuroscience.)

I had several methodological issues, or rather issues of interperetation. The behavioral experiments in mice relied on the elevated-T-maze. In this experimental protocol, the 'learned' fear behavior is passive, the 'insticntive' fear behavior is active. In the human experiments the learned fear is neither activity nor passivity, but a physiological response.

These experiments fail to distinguish betwen their hypothesis and the straightfoward alternative that the activity of the DRN promotes passivity, which is also consistent with more recent work implicating it in learned-helplessness.

In animal models of stress related to inhibition of responses serotonin is implicated as anxiogenic. In animal models in which the animal escapes or avoids aversive events, serotonin is implicated as anxiolytic. (In that it slows the learning of escape/avoidance - I am not sure this qualifies as anxiolytic.)

Personally I think there is no contradiction here to resolve: there seems to be a general theme of 5ht promoting passivity in response to a threat. Perhaps in the original deakin/graeff paper they go into more detail about what remains to be resolved here.

Graeff brings up deakin-5ht-1991 to reconcile these results. This hypothesis suggests that the DRN -> amygdala, frontal cortex pathway promotes (learned) escape behaviors. (directly contradicting learned-helplessness-insights-from-neuroscience) This pathway they implicate in general anxiety disorder. The DRN-periventricular/periaqueductal gray pathway inhibits inborn (instinctive) fight or flight reactions. This pathway they propose relates to panic disorder.

The pathway connecting median raphe nucleus to the hippocampus promotes reisistance to chronic stress. It dissociates aversive events from appetitive and social behaviors, allowing the organism to lead a normal life despite constant adversity. (This is interesting ,as it is consistent with the idea of ht2c receptors mediating unlearning. However, they suggest 5HT1a. I think their reasoning has to do with the fact that depressed patients show increased 5HT2c expression, while 5HT1A is known to interact with glucocorticoids, and impair LTP in the hippocampus (5).). Also interesting is that the dissociative plasticity you would expect in the hippocampus is actually documented in the frontal cortex, but not to my knowledge the hippocampus) They suggest that when this pathway is disrupted, depression occurs.

They use an elevated T maze, which is an elevated T-shaped platform where in the lower arm the rat is enclosed and feels safe, while feeling exposed in the other arms. The rat is first placed in the safe space to measure their avoidance of the exposed areas. Then, their time to actively escape to safety from the exposed arms is measured.

The idea is that hiding passively in the body of the T is a learned fear (because until the rat runs out and finds itself exposed, it doesn't know the rest of the t is danger), while sitting out on the open arms is innate fear, and seeking cover is instinctive fight or flight behavior. I'm not certain about this way of viewing the different arms. The clearest difference is active v.s. passive, learned vs instinctive is speculative, in my view.

Diazepam and 5HT1a ligand ipsapirone impaired inhibitory avoidance (hiding in the safe arm), while leaving escape behavior untouched. This is taken as validation of the experimental protocol.

They injected the 5HT1A receptor agonist 8-OH-DPAT, into the DRN, which should decrease 5HT firing rate. This impaired the passive, learned fear (consistent with my alternative that 5ht promotes passivity as well as their hypothesis). However, it did not increase escape behavior, which is what they expected.

To see whether increasing 5HT activity would increase hiding and decrease escape, they tried three methods.

They injected FG-7142, or kainic-acid into DRN or administered D-fenfluramine. FG-7142 enhanced inhibitory avoidance but did not affect active escape. kainic-acid enhanced inhibitory avoidance and impaired active escape. D-fenfluramine enhanced inhibitory avoidance, and reduced escape speed.

"The negative results with 8- OH-DPAT and FG 7142 on escape contrasts with the effec- tiveness of both drugs on avoidance. This may be an indi- cation that the DRN-periventricular 5-HT system has no inhibitory tone, and acts on DPAG neurons controlling de- fense and aversion in a phasic way. Indeed, this hypothesis has already been suggested on the basis of the absence of behavioral changes following the microinjection of 5-HT2A,2C receptor blockers into the DPAG (35)"

They went on to test their hypothesis with human volunteers. They gave them a pavlovian fear task (learned fear) and a simulated public speaking task (instinctive fear), and gave them either drugs or a placebo. The drugs they used were D-fenfluramine, m-CPP, ritanserin, SR-46349B.

On the basis of their hypothesis, they expected that agonists would increase fear in the pavlovian task and reduce it in the public speaking task, while antagonists would have the opposite effect.

D-fenfluramine reduced anxiety in the public speaking task and enhanced conditioned fear. m-CPP did not affect public speaking, but did enhance conditioned fear. ritanserin and SR-46349B both increased anxiety in the conditioned task, while the former reduced fear in public speaking and the latter did not. (For ritanserin, this apparently parallels the aggravation of panic disorder by the drug - but doesn't that contradict their hypothesis? Panic seems to me to be the most obvious example of an unconditioned fear response? maybe they meant in the sense that their conditioned task measured a physiological response?)

They frame results from learned-helplessness models as consistent with their hypothesis. In particular, the increased conditioned freezing is consistent with their model, but those experiments have no unconditioned fear to compare to. The impairment of learning however is not directly evident from their model: in particular its alleviation by DRN lesion. They do suggest on the basis of that that two different pathways - PAG and Amygdala - modulate the freezing and learning impairment. (escape defecit required targeting the DRN, amygdala only hit increased freezing)

On the basis of these results they propose that D-fendfluramine reduces panic attacks in panic disorder by inhibiting instinctive fear responses.

They replicated experiments showing that lesioning MRN increased ulcer formation, and extended this to show immunideficiency.

Another experiment found that rats which exhibited the most resilience to stress had stronger behavioral responses to 5-MeO-DMT, and 8-OH-DPAT showed improvements in resilience. This does not specifically implicate the MRN however, as adminisration was sytemic. This could also be due to reduction in DRN activity.

They proposed (from 16) that glucocorticoids sensitize and up-regulate 5HT1ARs short-term, but down-regulate them if chronic.

They associate 5HT2CRs with anxiety, and based on (5) say 5HT2CRs oppose 5HT1ARs.

Takeaways

  • Graeff raises an interesting point about serotonin in anxiety. It has been suggested that the anxiolytic/antidepressant effects of serotonin are due a reduction in serotonin release due to 5HT1a receptor activation. Graeff claims that electrophysiological and microdyalisis studies show this effect to be short-lived. I haven't seen this point brought up in later literature. Was it overlooked, or overturned?
  • the claim that chronic SSRI administration downregulates prefrontal expression of 5HT2C showed up here again. It linked again to https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/0006322388900911?via%3Dihub. I cannot find this claim in this article, and either way it is odd that graeff cites only the review and not the stud(ies) that found the downregulation. As I understand it, this article proposed this as a possibility, rather than a confirmed observation. I suspect this claim made its way, mis-citation and all, to wikipedia from this article. I can revisit this topic with papers summarized here https://elicit.com/review/d23a00a0-b5d6-44a0-9363-7ee41d2422ec
  • learned helplessness models of depression also show potentiated fear condtioning as well as escape deficits (this is shown in increased freezing behavior - not sure these are really separate)
  • (48) showed that lesioning MRN, but not DRN increased stress-induced ulcers (does this have to do with the hippocampal pathway, though?)
  • several studies show that 5HT is released in the hippocampus during stress
  • adrenal corticosteroids increase the consolidation of stressful memories
  • (33, 12, 13, 14, 85) systemic and hippocampal 5ht potentiation disrupt behavior acquisition
  • (109, 21, 94) 5HT1AR inhibits long-term-potentiation in the hippocampus
  • (17) 5HT1a receptors are reduced in suicide victms
  • (31, 81, 84, 101) glucocorticoids are more present depressed patients, and drugs that inhibit cortisol synthesis improve depression
  • (51) 5HT2CRs show increased expresion in depressed patients
  • (64, 72, 104) scress and glucocorticoids increase 5HT2CR expression.
